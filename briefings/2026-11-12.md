## 1. SLOVENIA

**Local elections Sunday — Ljubljana and Maribor are the races to watch.**
Slovenia votes for mayors and municipal councils on Sunday 15 November, with runoffs on 29 November where no candidate clears 50%. In Ljubljana, Zoran Janković is seeking what he says will be his last term after nearly two decades, against a field including Luka Mesec (Levica) and independent influencer Aleksandar Repić. Maribor is wide open: incumbent Saša Arsenovič is not running, leaving ten candidates, among them Katja Koren (Demokrati/SDS/NSi/Fokus) and Matevž Frangež (SD/Levica).

**2027–2028 budgets reach the finance committee tomorrow — and the framing split is sharp.**
Parliament's finance committee takes up the two-year budget documents on 13–14 November. Gibanje Svoboda had demanded the government correct the documents by today, arguing the fiscal hole is widening even with stronger growth and revenues.
*Framing disagreement — the clearest of the week:* Finance Minister Andrej Šircelj says public finances are stable and blames "inherited financial disarray"; the unions call the draft "a new ZUJF," warning of cuts to social transfers, families and the public sector; pro-government **nova24tv** frames the same period as the economy "returning to stronger growth." Both sides can cite real numbers — UMAR forecasts 3.8% growth this year (vs 1.5% last), while the budget ran a ~€1.2bn deficit over ten months, with a €2.2bn (2.9% of GDP) year-end deficit projected.

**Intervention-law referendum set for 6 December after a reversal.**
The finance committee approved the decree calling a legislative referendum on the Act on intervention measures, with the plenary to confirm. Note the sequence: the committee had *earlier* backed the view that a referendum was inadmissible — the legal service argued the law concerns taxes and compulsory levies — and the unions, who filed 40,000+ certified signatures on 9 October, challenged that at the Constitutional Court before the position flipped. The Finance Ministry puts the law's cost at ~€647m/year in forgone revenue; the government says it introduces no new tax and cuts no pension, while the opposition calls it "a law for the wealthy."

**Janša escalates against Inštitut 8. marec; coalition moves on campaign-finance law.**
The prime minister published a graphic of the institute's funding flows on X, calling the arrangement unconstitutional and illegal. In parallel, coalition MPs and Resni.ca filed an amendment to the election and referendum campaign act barring legal entities funded from abroad in the last five years from organising campaigns. *Framing:* Siol presents this as "panic" at the institute; EDRi (an advocacy group, not a neutral observer) characterises the broader pattern as a crackdown on independent civil society.

*Also:* police to be the first group out of the common public-sector pay system; a Ljubljana court ordered Bojan Požar to remove a video post of Luka Mesec under threat of escalating fines; academic and former politician Matjaž Kmecl has died.

## 2. GLOBAL

**Abu Dhabi talks end with a prisoner deal and little else.**
The first US-mediated Russia–Ukraine talks in nine months ran 10–11 November and closed with agreement on a major POW release but no breakthrough on territory or security guarantees.
*Wire vs. other framing:* **Reuters** led on both sides reporting "some progress" while US mediators cited a lack of trust and persistent differences, and noted Russia's delegation declined to meet European and NATO officials present in Abu Dhabi. **AP** led instead on the war itself — no sign the fighting is abating, with new long-range strikes by both sides during the talks. Euronews emphasised Zelenskyy welcoming a "change in approach" by US mediators; Lavrov rejected a truce outright, calling it breathing space for Ukraine, while von der Leyen said broader security talks should follow a ceasefire, not precede it. Further talks are expected next week.

**Strait of Hormuz: more tanker fires claimed, none independently verified.**
Iran's IRGC says tankers have burned in a series of incidents in early November — two after striking naval mines on 8 November, five plus a tugboat claimed by 5 November — with Tehran saying vessels had navigation and GPS off and were on an "unauthorized" route.
*This is the week's biggest sourcing caveat:* every one of these claims originates with the IRGC or Iranian state media, with no maritime-authority, shipping-operator or US confirmation in any coverage I could reach. What is independently measurable: the strait is effectively closed to commercial traffic — IMF PortWatch logged 2 transits on 25 October against an 85/day pre-crisis baseline. Iran's security chief says it stays closed until the US meets seven conditions; CENTCOM reportedly disabled a cargo vessel in the Gulf of Oman on 8 November for attempting to break the blockade.

**Nvidia's board weighs removing Jensen Huang.**
The **NYT** reported a relationship between Huang and Kimberly Powell, Nvidia's VP of health care, who reports to him, described by five employees. The **WSJ** reported first and went further: the board is preparing to remove him and could meet as soon as Wednesday. Huang acknowledged infidelity on Sunday, and Nvidia opened an investigation into reports he was targeted by a foreign intelligence operation; **Fortune** frames it around a separate affair with a woman US officials deemed a Chinese spy. The board retained Wachtell Lipton. Stock fell 8% after hours. **Nothing is confirmed** — Nvidia, Huang and Powell have not confirmed the relationship, and no removal had been announced as of the latest coverage. Succession is the real exposure: Huang has said he doesn't believe in succession planning.

**House Republicans kill the Hegseth impeachment push.**
The House voted 211–204 on 10 November to refer Rep. Thomas Massie's impeachment articles against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to the Judiciary Committee, ending them for the 119th Congress without a vote on the articles. Massie was the only Republican voting against; 11 Democrats missed the vote — enough to have defeated the motion 215–211 — with several blaming traffic, drawing public criticism from Rep. Kamlager-Dove. *Framing note:* some outlets call it "tabling," others "referral"; the procedural effect is identical. Democrats take both chambers in January, and Jeffries said "this is not the end."

**COP31 leaders' segment closes in Antalya with the big questions open.**
The high-level segment ended today; negotiations run to 20 November, so there is no outcome text yet. Guterres proposed a "Climate Justice Compact" for fossil-fuel transition in developing countries. The leaders of China, the US and India — the three largest emitters — are absent, as are most G7 leaders. The concrete deliverable so far is financial, not normative: a record $1.6bn AIIB–Türkiye agreement supporting reforms under Türkiye's climate law, plus an Azerbaijan–Georgia–Türkiye–Bulgaria clean electricity corridor deal. An EU official has already warned that appetite for new finance commitments is limited.

*Also:* a US soldier was arrested in England on suspicion of spying for Russia; three people were charged in the US over an alleged plot including a British airbase.

## 3. SCIENCE & HEALTH

**CONFIRMED — FDA warns GLP-1 drugs carry a risk of a rare blinding eye condition.**
On 9 November the FDA required manufacturers to inform patients and physicians that GLP-1 drugs carry a potential risk of NAION, which causes sudden vision loss and in severe cases blindness. There are no proven treatments. This is a regulatory action, not a study finding — the firmest item in this section.

**CONFIRMED — California voters approve $8.4bn in research bonds (Prop 38).**
AP projected passage on 10 November of the largest sum for science ever authorised by a US public vote: $8.4bn in general obligation bonds for immunology and immunotherapy, half to a UC-affiliated institute and half to grants, with half the research money reserved for cancer, heart disease and Alzheimer's. Billionaire retired surgeon Gary Michelson was the principal backer. Cost: $500–600m/year for roughly 20 years. *Caveat:* AP projection, not a certified count. Nature's coverage notes the measure drew criticism despite passing — the concentration of half the money in one UCLA-affiliated institute is the sore point.

**COMPANY ANNOUNCEMENT, not a verified paper — DeepMind releases AlphaGenome 2.**
Announced 11 November: a next-generation DNA sequence model predicting which human cell types a variant acts in, trained on 107m+ single-cell profiles across 68 tissues, claiming nearly twice as many expression-altering variants identified as its predecessor. Available free for non-commercial research via API. Treat the performance numbers as DeepMind's own — I could not confirm a peer-reviewed AlphaGenome 2 paper, only the blog and news coverage. The original AlphaGenome was published in Nature in January 2026.

**Ebola in the DRC is now among the largest outbreaks on record — and the counts don't reconcile.**
The Bundibugyo-strain outbreak declared on 15 May 2026, a PHEIC and the DRC's 17th, has reportedly passed 11,000 cases with ~5,400 deaths across eight provinces, with spread to Kenya and Nigeria. *Explicit discrepancy:* those totals come from press reports and government data (one count: 11,042 cases / 5,387 deaths); the most recent WHO Disease Outbreak News I could reach covers data only through early October, and Wikipedia lists 11,073 confirmed as of 22 October. No November WHO situation report surfaced. There is **no licensed vaccine or specific treatment for the Bundibugyo species** — candidates are in testing — and WHO cites armed conflict, poor roads, a strained health system, community mistrust and health-worker shortages as constraints.

**PRELIMINARY — chemically confirmed blood traces claimed in 115-million-year-old dinosaur bones.**
Nature reports researchers finding chemically confirmed traces of ancient blood, which would challenge assumptions about how long biological molecules survive. Extraordinary-claim territory; treat as a single finding pending replication.

*Also in health policy:* the Senate health committee has scheduled next week's vote on FDA commissioner nominee Heidi Overton, with several Republicans including Sen. Cassidy publicly undecided; STAT reports the most-favored-nation pricing policy is pushing drugmakers to rethink when and where they launch abroad. At AHA 2026, researchers presented lessons from high-profile neutral trials — including why a cholesterol-lowering drug class failed to show benefit.
